# How to Find US Laws by the Day They Take Effect

If you need to know which new rules bind you on a particular date, searching by bill number is the slow way. Bills are numbered by session, renamed as they move, and often pass with a date far from the one people quote in news. A faster method is to work backwards from the date you care about and use a source that keeps effective dates as a first-class field.

## Start with the calendar, not the bill

The [State Law Watch home page](https://laws.resuhive.com/) lists 3,216 laws and rules that have a confirmed effective date. Each entry is written in plain English and links to the official text, so you can jump from a summary to the underlying document in one click. The site covers federal rules from the Federal Register, which are public domain, and state bills from LegiScan, used under CC BY 4.0 with attribution. Knowing the source matters because it tells you where to verify a detail before you rely on it.

## Pick a year, then a month

The [2027 laws page](https://laws.resuhive.com/laws/2027/) groups 56 confirmed changes by the month they take effect. January 2027 carries thirty-nine of them, February fourteen, June one, and July two. That distribution is useful on its own: it shows how heavily the calendar clusters at the start of the year. Click a rule to see its publication date, its effective date, the agency or legislature behind it, and the official notice.

## Cross-check by topic

Sometimes you do not have a date at all, only a subject. Topic pages solve that. The [minimum wage topic](https://laws.resuhive.com/topic/minimum-wage/) gathers 47 confirmed changes about wages, pay, overtime, and compensation, each with its own effective date and source. Recent entries include a Federal-State Unemployment Compensation data rule effective November 16, 2026, and Medicare FY 2027 hospice wage index updates effective October 1, 2026. Combining a topic view with a date view catches rules you would miss by scanning one state only.

## Confirm on the official notice

A summary is a starting point, not a substitute for the source. Before you act, open the official text linked from the detail page and check the effective date there. This is especially important for state bills marked TBD, where no official date has been published yet. A site that marks a date as pending is doing you a favor; a site that fills the gap with a guess is not.

## A short routine

1. Decide the date or subject you care about.
2. Open the year or topic page and scan the confirmed dates.
3. Read the plain-English summary for the closest matches.
4. Open the official text to verify the date.
5. Recheck close to the date, because schedules move.

That routine takes a few minutes and removes most of the guesswork from reading legal news.
